I study the neural mechanisms of speech motor control: how the brain monitors its own voice, corrects speech errors, and why this system fails in speech disorders such as stuttering. I combine task and resting-state fMRI, structural MRI, EEG and non-invasive brain stimulation with multivariate pattern analysis and effective-connectivity modelling. My work falls into four connected strands.

Sensorimotor control of speech
------

Speaking depends on continuously comparing what we intend to say with what we hear ourselves say. My doctoral work at Oxford examined this using altered auditory feedback: when the sound of a speaker's own vowels is shifted in real time, they gradually adjust their articulation to compensate. I combined this approach with brain stimulation and MRI to study the contribution of the cerebellum and sensorimotor cortex to that adaptation.

In a preregistered study of 90 participants, we tested whether transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) over motor cortex or cerebellum enhances speech adaptation. The previously published effect did not replicate ([Yuan et al., 2025, *Cortex*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2025.09.007); [data and code](https://osf.io/9bswe/)). A behavioural study and a functional and structural MRI study followed, identifying cortical and cerebellar mechanisms of speech adaptation, and a collaboration with Beijing Normal University ran matched behavioural and fMRI protocols in the UK and China. My thesis is available from the [Oxford University Research Archive](https://doi.org/10.5287/ora-yr004dmp6).

Stuttering and speech disorders
------

I use structural and resting-state fMRI to characterise how brain structure, activity and connectivity differ in people who stutter, working with a large neuroimaging dataset on developmental stuttering (~300 participants). I'm also interested in whether non-invasive brain stimulation can support speech-motor control.

Language production in bilinguals
------

Bilingual speakers have to select the intended language and keep the other from interfering. Using fMRI, multivoxel pattern analysis and effective-connectivity modelling, I have mapped the brain networks behind this language control ([Yuan et al., 2021, *Brain Structure and Function*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00429-021-02218-7)), how they relate to domain-general cognitive control, and how they change with training and with language context. I have also used EEG to study how bilinguals track continuous speech in noise ([preregistration](https://osf.io/xyqfr/)).

The cerebellum in speech, language and reading
------

Across these strands I have a particular interest in the cerebellum. Combining fMRI, effective-connectivity modelling and brain stimulation (tDCS), my work examined how language control and domain-general cognitive control are organised within the cerebellum and its connections with the cerebral cortex ([Yuan et al., 2023, *Cerebral Cortex*](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhac051)). With collaborators, I proposed the cerebro-cerebellar mapping hypothesis, a model of the cerebellum's contribution to reading and to developmental dyslexia ([Li, Yuan et al., 2022, *Neuropsychologia*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2022.108231)).
