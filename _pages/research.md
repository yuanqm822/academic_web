I study how the brain controls speech and language, using MRI to measure brain function and structure and non-invasive brain stimulation to test whether speech production can be modulated. My work falls into four connected strands.

Sensorimotor control of speech
------

Speaking depends on continuously comparing what we intend to say with what we hear ourselves say. My doctoral work at Oxford examined this using altered auditory feedback: when the sound of a speaker's own vowels is shifted in real time, they gradually adjust their articulation to compensate. I combined this approach with brain stimulation and functional MRI to study the contribution of the cerebellum and sensorimotor cortex to that adaptation.

We found that transcranial direct current stimulation over motor cortex or cerebellum did not enhance speech adaptation, failing to replicate an earlier report ([Yuan et al., 2025, *Cortex*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2025.09.007)). My thesis is available from the [Oxford University Research Archive](https://doi.org/10.5287/ora-yr004dmp6).

Speech and language disorders
------

I use MRI to investigate how brain activity, structure and connectivity differ in people with speech and language disorders, including people who stutter, and whether non-invasive brain stimulation can be used to support speech-motor control.

Language production in bilinguals
------

Bilingual speakers have to select the intended language and keep the other from interfering. Using MRI and brain stimulation, I study the brain networks behind this language control, how they relate to domain-general cognitive control, and how they change with training and with language context.

The cerebellum in speech and reading
------

Across these strands I have a particular interest in the cerebellum. With collaborators, I examine its role in language control, speech and reading, including how cerebellar regions work together with the cerebral cortex.
